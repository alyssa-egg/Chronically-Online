01. The Lore Files

Internet history · Fandom deep dives

Long-form, magazine-style stories about things like:

The rise and fall of YouTube friend groups.

Why 2014 One Direction fandom still lives in people's heads.

Interactive twist: Every article has a clickable timeline, related references, and a “go deeper” path into connected stories.

05. The Group Chat

Community · Opinions · Reader participation

This is where the publication feels less like a traditional magazine and more like a shared corner of the internet.

Ideas include reader-submitted internet memories, anonymous fandom confessions, “what was your first fandom?” prompts, monthly favorites, and community polls.

Interactive twist: Readers contribute to future features. For example, their memories of 2014 Tumblr become part of a community-built time capsule.

3. What would actually make your publication different?
I would give every story three layers:

Read it

A genuinely good editorial piece with your perspective, research, images, and cultural analysis.

Explore it

Timelines, interactive images, clickable references, polls, music connections, and visual comparisons.

Keep it

Readers save favorite stories and discoveries to a personal archive, build collections, and revisit their own internet interests.

For example, an article about the 2014 Tumblr era wouldn't end after someone reads it. They could explore the timeline, identify references in a moodboard, listen to a connected playlist, and save the story to their personal archive.

That is a much more compelling experience than adding a quiz at the bottom of an article.

5. How this could become a real creative brand
I'd also think beyond the website itself. The editorial concept could support:

A weekly internet culture newsletter: five things worth knowing, a deep cut, and something readers might have missed.

Instagram and TikTok editorial posts: timelines, mini-documentaries, aesthetic breakdowns, and “remember when?” features that lead back to the site.

Reader-powered archives: people submit old fan-account memories, forgotten trends, screenshots they have permission to share, and personal stories.

Special digital issues: themed editions like The Tumblr Years, The Pop Girl Internet, or The YouTube Friend Group Files.

Curated playlists: soundtracks attached to specific eras, fandoms, and stories.

You could build a portfolio of original editorial work while growing an actual publication with a recognizable voice.

One thing I'd be careful about: if you cover current news, verify it and cite sources; if you're documenting older internet culture, distinguish documented history from fan recollections and opinion. That editorial credibility can be part of what sets the site apart.

My biggest recommendation
Build it as an editorial publication first, with an interactive archive as its signature feature.

I wouldn't try to become a general celebrity-news outlet. I'd own the intersection of internet history, music, fandom, creator culture, aesthetics, and the memories people share online.

The most interesting question for every story would be: What does this moment tell us about internet culture — and what else does it connect to?

That gives you a clearer identity than either a standard magazine or a trivia site. It also means you can keep publishing new stories without needing every new feature to be a game.
